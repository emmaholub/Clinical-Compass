Clinical Compass

Final project report · Emma Holub

Clinical Compass translates a new diagnosis into plain language, explains guideline-linked treatment options with traceable safety evidence, prepares questions for a doctor visit, and finds larger recruiting US clinical trials. It is patient education, not clinical decision-making.

1. Motivation

A newly diagnosed patient often has to interpret unfamiliar medical language, compare treatment information scattered across labels and studies, and decide what to ask at a short appointment. Clinical Compass gives patients a calm, structured starting point while keeping the clinician in the loop. The product is designed for patients and caregivers rather than clinicians.

Current workflow: search engines, general chatbots, drug labels, and clinical-trial pages. That workflow is fragmented and can blur the difference between evidence, a safety warning, and advice. Compass keeps sources visible and labels uncertainty.

2. What is new

This is not simply chat with a PDF. For every lookup, a disease-specific guideline plan drives the treatment list; FDA/DailyMed labels and PubMed abstracts are then retrieved for those drugs; exact quotes are checked against the cited URL; and recruiting US trials are filtered by status, phase, location, and enrollment. Failed evidence is shown as a limitation instead of being silently replaced with an empty success.

Compared with a general chatbot, the useful distinction is the evidence pipeline and patient-friendly presentation. Compared with a drug-label site, Compass connects the diagnosis, guideline role, safety context, doctor questions, and trials in one short flow.

3. Agents and architecture

The boss normalizes the disease and runs three first-wave workers concurrently: overview, standard of care, and trials. If the guideline plan succeeds, two second-wave workers run concurrently: standard-treatment safety and alternative-treatment safety. Every worker returns a strict Pydantic model.

The model receives retrieved evidence as untrusted text and is instructed to treat it as data, not instructions. Timeouts, request limits, bounded retries, and partial-result errors protect the user and budget.

6. Safety

  • Prompt injection: retrieved pages are explicitly untrusted data; the model is told never to follow instructions in sources.
  • Unsupported medical claims: source URLs and contiguous quotes are validated; unsupported numbers become null and are explained in the UI.
  • Wrong-label risk: brand/innovator labels are preferred, combination products are excluded from single-drug searches, and DailyMed is a fallback.
  • Malfunction and overspending: worker timeouts, evidence request caps, two-request LLM limits, and an anonymous per-connection search limit bound failure and cost.

Next steps: user testing, US guideline coverage, persistent rate limits and budget controls, clinician review, and a larger evidence index.
